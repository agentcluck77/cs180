Overview

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed Russia between 1907 and 1915 by exposing three glass plates of the same scene through red, green, and blue filters. Each digitized scan is one tall grayscale image with all three plates stacked B / G / R top to bottom. For this project I wrote a pipeline that splits each scan into its three channels, searches for the (dy, dx) shift that best lines G and R up with B, and merges the result into a single color photo.

Single-Scale Alignment

I started with the two small JPEGs, since a bug here is a lot faster to spot on a 390px-wide image than a 9800px one. For each channel I exhaustively try every displacement in a ±15 pixel window and score it with a matching metric, computed only on the interior of the image. The outer 10% gets cropped before scoring, since plate borders and scan artifacts would otherwise dominate the score and drag the search toward the wrong answer. Two metrics:

  • SSD / L2 norm: sqrt(sum((image1 - image2)²)), lower is better.
  • NCC: dot product of the two mean-subtracted, unit-normalized images, higher is better.

Both metrics agree exactly on all three low-resolution images:

Multi-Scale Pyramid Alignment

A ±15px brute-force search works fine on a small JPEG, but it falls apart on the full-resolution .tif scans (up to ~9800×3800), where the true offset can be well over 150px, and the exhaustive search is both too slow at that resolution and too narrow to even find the right answer. So instead I built a coarse-to-fine image pyramid: shrink both channels by 2× (cv2.resize with INTER_AREA), recursively solve the same problem on the smaller image, then scale that estimate back up and refine it with a small local search at the current resolution. This bottoms out once an image is under 400px, where a direct search (radius 30) runs as the base case. Each image, no matter the resolution, aligns in under 2 seconds.

SSD vs. NCC: They Don't Always Agree

On most images SSD and NCC land on the exact same answer, which was reassuring the first time I checked. church.tif was the one that broke that streak, and the reason why was surprising to me.

Why does SSD get it wrong here?

My first guess was that the search window was too small and SSD had just gotten stuck at the edge of it. But widening the radius from 15 to 60 gave the exact same answer. The real cause was that the B and R plates for this particular scan differ noticeably in average brightness (mean pixel value ~47000 vs. ~33000 out of 65535). L2 norms punishes brightness mismatch just as much as structural mismatch, so SSD can get pulled toward a shift that happens to average out the brightness gap rather than the shift that actually lines up the church and the water. NCC mean-subtracts and normalizes both images before comparing, so a flat brightness offset disappears entirely and only the shape of the image matters.

Per the assignment, every image above is still documented with plain L2/NCC results even where a more advanced metric (gradient-based, see below) was used for the final output.

Failure Case: emir.tif

emir.tif (the Emir of Bukhara) is a difficult case. NCC on raw pixel values here seems to not work. This isn't a brightness offset issue like church.tif. The R-channel plate simply captured different detail than B and G. Raw-pixel alignment locks onto R offset (416, −455), a shift bigger than the image itself is wide.

How I fixed it

This is why i attempted to build the "Better Features" bells & whistle below: instead of scoring alignment on raw pixel brightness, I score it on Sobel edge/gradient magnitude. Edges (where the door frame ends, where a fold in the robe falls) are far more consistent across channels than raw intensity ever is, even when the channels disagree on exposure. Switching to gradients fixed emir.tif outright, and I liked the result enough that it's now the default alignment strategy for every image on this page, not just this one. With it applied everywhere, all 14 provided images align correctly, with no remaining failures.

Bells & Whistles

1. Better Features

Covered above (see Failure Case)

2. Automatic Cropping

I didn't want to just chop off a fixed percentage from each side, so instead I detect the border by computing gradient energy per row/column: real photo content has texture (high gradient energy), while the plate's border and the color fringe left behind by the np.roll wrap-around is close to flat (low gradient energy). Each edge gets trimmed inward until the energy crosses 10% of that edge's peak, capped at 15% of the image so an edge case image can't trick it into cropping the whole photo.

3. Automatic Contrasting

The 0.5th percentile of intensity maps to 0, the 99.5th maps to 255. I used percentiles instead of the true min/max after noticing a couple of scans have a single stray dead or hot pixel that would otherwise anchor the whole stretch and barely move anything else.

4. Automatic White Balance

Gray-world assumption: scale each channel so its mean matches the average of all three channel means. Simple to implement, and it does a decent job pulling an overall color cast back toward neutral.

5. Better Color Mapping

Prokudin-Gorskii's glass filters weren't perfectly narrowband, so each plate picks up some spectral "bleed" from its neighbors, muddying the final colors.
